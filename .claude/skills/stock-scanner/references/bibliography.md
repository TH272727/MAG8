The hard gates and factor screens apply published, replicated results — the thresholds are the literature's, not ours.

- **Piotroski, J. D. (2000).** Value Investing: The Use of Historical Financial Statement Information to Separate Winners from Losers. *Journal of Accounting Research, 38 (Supplement), 1–41.* <https://doi.org/10.2307/2672906>
  A simple nine-signal fundamental-strength score (the F-Score) raised a value investor's mean annual return by at least 7.5 percentage points; a long-high/short-low F-Score strategy earned ~23% annualized over 1976–1996. The F-Score is the financial-strength gate — a low score can veto a candidate regardless of story.
- **Altman, E. I. (1968).** Financial Ratios, Discriminant Analysis and the Prediction of Corporate Bankruptcy. *The Journal of Finance, 23(4), 589–609.* <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1968.tb00843.x>
  A five-ratio discriminant model (the Z-Score) correctly classified 95% of the original sample as bankrupt or non-bankrupt one year before failure. The distress-zone veto uses Altman's own zone boundaries — a balance sheet in the distress zone fails the gate.
- **Fama, E. F., & French, K. R. (2015).** A Five-Factor Asset Pricing Model. *Journal of Financial Economics, 116(1), 1–22.* <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2014.10.010>
  Adding profitability and investment factors to market, size, and value better explains the cross-section of average stock returns. Grounds treating profitability and investment discipline as compensated factors in the quality screen.
- **Asness, C. S., Frazzini, A., & Pedersen, L. H. (2019).** Quality Minus Junk. *Review of Accounting Studies, 24(1), 34–112.* <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11142-018-9470-2>
  Stocks of profitable, growing, safe, well-managed companies earned significantly higher risk-adjusted returns than junk across 24 countries. The quality dimension of the screen — quality is priced, but not fully.
- **Novy-Marx, R. (2013).** The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium. *Journal of Financial Economics, 108(1), 1–28.* <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2013.01.003>
  Gross profits-to-assets predicts average returns about as powerfully as book-to-market and adds information beyond valuation. Why the screen insists on profitable quality inside cheapness rather than cheapness alone.
- **George, T. J., & Hwang, C.-Y. (2004).** The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing. *The Journal of Finance, 59(5), 2145–2176.* <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00695.x>
  Nearness to the 52-week high explains most momentum profits and forecasts returns better than past returns themselves. Part of the momentum/confirmation check that separates a beaten-down bargain from a falling knife.
- **Bernard, V. L., & Thomas, J. K. (1989).** Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift: Delayed Price Response or Risk Premium? *Journal of Accounting Research, 27 (Supplement), 1–36.* <https://doi.org/10.2307/2491062>
  Prices keep drifting in the direction of an earnings surprise for months after the announcement — a delayed response, not a risk premium. Earnings-surprise drift as a catalyst-confirmation signal.
