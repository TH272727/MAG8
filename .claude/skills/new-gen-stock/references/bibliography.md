The hunt for the next mega-caps is grounded in how equity returns actually concentrate, and the DNA scorecard operationalizes the standard strategy literature rather than inventing criteria.

- **Bessembinder, H. (2018).** Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills? *Journal of Financial Economics, 129(3), 440–457.* <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2018.06.004>
  Since 1926, the best-performing ~4% of US listed companies account for the entire net wealth creation of the stock market over Treasury bills; the top ~90 firms account for over half, and 57% of individual stocks underperformed T-bills over their lifetimes. The rationale for the scout's whole mandate: returns concentrate in a handful of compounders, so the game is finding them early — as a basket, with honest odds.
- **Fahlenbrach, R. (2009).** Founder-CEOs, Investment Decisions, and Stock Market Performance. *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 44(2), 439–466.* <https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109009090139>
  An equal-weighted portfolio of founder-CEO firms earned a benchmark-adjusted ~8% annually (1993–2002), with founder-led firms investing more in R&D and capex. The founder-led / reinvestment dimensions of the DNA scorecard.
- **Helmer, H. (2016).** 7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy. *Deep Strategy LLC.* <https://7powers.com/>
  Durable excess returns require one of seven structural Powers — scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource, or process power. The moat and counter-positioning dimensions ('counter-positioning' is Helmer's term).
- **Rogers, E. M. (1962).** Diffusion of Innovations. *Free Press (5th ed. 2003).* <https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Diffusion-of-Innovations-5th-Edition/Everett-M-Rogers/9780743222099>
  Innovations spread along a predictable S-shaped adoption curve, from innovators and early adopters through the majority. The S-curve timing dimension — the scout hunts for companies near the knee of adoption.
- **Foster, R. N. (1986).** Innovation: The Attacker's Advantage. *Summit Books.* <https://archive.org/details/innovationattack0000fost>
  Technologies improve along S-curves that hit physical limits, at which point attackers on a new S-curve gain a structural advantage over defending incumbents. Why the scout prefers attackers riding fresh S-curves over incumbents defending old ones.
- **Christensen, C. M. (1997).** The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail. *Harvard Business School Press.* <https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=46>
  Well-managed incumbents rationally serving their best customers systematically lose to disruptive entrants that start at the low end and improve. The incumbent-bind logic inside the counter-positioning dimension.
- **Moore, G. A. (1991).** Crossing the Chasm: Marketing and Selling High-Tech Products to Mainstream Customers. *HarperBusiness.* <https://geoffreyamoore.com/book/crossing-the-chasm/>
  A chasm separates early adopters from the mainstream majority; technology companies succeed or fail on whether they can bridge it. The adoption-gap realism in S-curve timing — early traction is not the same as mainstream arrival.
